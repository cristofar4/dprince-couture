The collection

Nine pieces, cut once

AW26 is deliberately small. Nine garments and three accessories, made in quantities set by how much embroidery one pair of hands can finish in a season.

The house makes fourteen ceremonial agbada a year. That ceiling is not a marketing position — it is the honest capacity of the atelier, and it decides everything else about how the collection is built.

The ceremonial line

Cut for the moment everyone is watching

A ceremonial agbada has no fastening to hold it and no waist to sit on — three metres of cloth hanging from a line forty-five centimetres wide. Whatever that shoulder does, the hem repeats, amplified.

The house solves it with a bias-cut linen strip eased into the seam by a millimetre and a half. It is the entire difference between grandeur and costume.

In the atelier

Eleven days of thread

The embroidery panel on a ceremonial agbada is worked by one pair of hands, never two. Two embroiderers produce two tensions, and on flat silver thread against a woven ground that difference reads as a ripple across the finished panel. You would not be able to name what was wrong with it. You would simply find it cheap.

01

Cloth

Hand-loomed in Ìsẹ̀yìn on a narrow loom, in strips a hand’s width across, then joined.

02

Pattern

Drafted to the client, chalked by hand, and filed under their name for every future commission.

03

Embroidery

Silver thread on the ground cloth rather than an applied panel, so it will not lift in twenty years.

04

Finish

Seams bound, never overlocked — so a tailor who has never seen the garment can let it out.
